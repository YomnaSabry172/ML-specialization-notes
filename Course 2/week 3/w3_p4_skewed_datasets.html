1. Why accuracy fails on skewed data

A skewed dataset is one where the ratio of positive to negative examples is far from 50/50 — e.g. a rare disease that only 0.5% of patients actually have.

Suppose your classifier achieves 1% error (99% accuracy) on this task. That sounds great — until you compare it to a trivial baseline: a program that just always prints y = 0, never diagnosing anyone. Since only 0.5% of patients actually have the disease, that dumb program is right 99.5% of the time — lower error than your trained model.

"Always predict 0" → 99.5% accuracy (0.5% error)
Your trained model → 99% accuracy (1% error)
Why this matters: the "always predict 0" program is completely useless — it never diagnoses a single patient. Yet it beats your real model on plain accuracy. This means accuracy alone can't tell you whether 99% is a good score or a bad one, and it can't reliably rank two real algorithms against each other either (is 99.2% better or worse than 99.6%, if one of them is secretly just guessing the majority class more often?).

You need a metric that specifically penalizes never predicting the rare class — that's what precision and recall are built for.

2. The confusion matrix

To compute precision and recall, first tabulate every prediction into a 2×2 confusion matrix: actual class (1 or 0) across the top, predicted class (1 or 0) down the side.

CellMeaningCount (example)
True Positive (TP)Predicted 1, actually 1 — correctly caught15
False Positive (FP)Predicted 1, actually 0 — false alarm5
False Negative (FN)Predicted 0, actually 1 — missed case10
True Negative (TN)Predicted 0, actually 0 — correctly cleared70
Why this matters: the confusion matrix separates "how many did we get right" into four distinct kinds of right/wrong, because for a rare condition the two kinds of mistakes (a false alarm vs. a missed diagnosis) have very different real-world costs — a single "accuracy" number blends them together and loses that distinction entirely.

3. Precision and recall, defined

By convention, y = 1 is the rare class you actually care about detecting.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)   =   15 / (15+5) = 0.75

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)   =   15 / (15+10) = 0.60
  • Precision — "Of all the patients we predicted have the disease, what fraction actually do?" (75% here — when this model raises an alarm, it's right 3 times out of 4.)
  • Recall — "Of all the patients who actually have the disease, what fraction did we correctly catch?" (60% here — the model finds 6 out of every 10 real cases.)
Analogy: think of a airport metal-detector alarm. Precision asks: of everyone the detector flags, how many are actually carrying something? Recall asks: of everyone actually carrying something, how many does the detector catch? A detector that's too twitchy has low precision (lots of false alarms); one that's too lax has low recall (lots of missed contraband).

4. How precision/recall catch the "always predict 0" cheat

Go back to the useless "always predict y = 0" program from Section 1. It never predicts positive, so TP = 0.

Recall = 0 / (0 + FN) = 0

Recall collapses to exactly zero — instantly exposing that this "model" never actually catches a single real case, no matter how good its accuracy looked. (Precision technically becomes 0/0 — undefined — but by convention is also just reported as 0 in this situation.)

Why this matters: this is the whole point of switching metrics. A model with zero precision or zero recall is immediately flagged as useless, which accuracy alone was completely blind to. Any genuinely useful model on a skewed dataset needs both numbers to be reasonably high — not just one.

5. Trading off precision and recall via the threshold

Logistic regression outputs f(x) between 0 and 1. Normally you threshold at 0.5: predict 1 if f(x) ≥ 0.5. But you can move that threshold — and doing so directly trades precision against recall.

Raising the threshold (e.g. to 0.7 or 0.9)

You now only predict "has the disease" when very confident. Fewer positive predictions overall, but the ones you do make are more trustworthy → higher precision, lower recall. This suits situations where treatment is invasive/expensive and you only want to act when quite sure.

Lowering the threshold (e.g. to 0.3)

You predict "has the disease" more liberally — "when in doubt, predict positive." You catch more true cases, but also generate more false alarms → lower precision, higher recall. This suits situations where a missed diagnosis is far worse than an unnecessary follow-up test.

Predict 1 if fw,b(x) ≥ threshold   (general form; 0.5 is just one choice of threshold)
Note: unlike choosing a model's parameters or hyperparameters, picking this threshold isn't something cross validation does for you automatically — it's a judgment call you make based on the real-world costs of a false positive vs. a false negative for your specific application. 6. Combining them: the F1 score

Sometimes you need to compare algorithms automatically rather than eyeball a curve — e.g. picking among 3 candidate models, each with its own precision/recall pair.

AlgorithmPrecisionRecallPlain average
10.50.40.45
20.70.10.40
30.021.00.51
Why the plain average fails: Algorithm 3 gets the highest average (0.51) — but its precision is 0.02, meaning it's predicting positive almost indiscriminately (this is essentially the "always predict 1" version of the earlier cheat). The plain average completely hides that this is a nearly useless model, because a very high recall can mathematically outweigh a near-zero precision in a simple average.

The fix is the F1 score — the harmonic mean of precision and recall, which specifically punishes a very low value in either metric rather than letting a high one compensate for it:

F1 = 1 / ( (1/2)(1/P + 1/R) )   =   2PR / (P + R)
AlgorithmPrecisionRecallF1 score
10.50.40.444
20.70.10.175
30.021.00.0392

Now Algorithm 1 clearly comes out on top — F1 pulls each score toward whichever of P or R is lower, so Algorithm 3's near-zero precision drags its F1 score down to nearly zero too, correctly flagging it as the weakest choice despite its perfect recall.

Why this matters: F1 gives you a single number to rank models by when precision and recall disagree about which is "best" — without falling for the same trap accuracy and the plain average both fall into: letting one extreme, easily-gamed metric hide behind a good-looking overall score.
Note: mathematically this formula is called the harmonic mean of P and R — a way of averaging that leans toward the smaller of the two values rather than treating them symmetrically like an arithmetic mean does. You don't need to memorize the term, just what it does.

7. Summary

MetricAnswersWeakness it fixes
AccuracyWhat fraction of predictions were correct overall?— (this is the metric that breaks on skewed data)
PrecisionWhen we predict positive, how often are we right?Catches a model that predicts positive too freely
RecallOf all real positives, how many did we catch?Catches a model that never predicts positive (the "always 0" cheat)
F1 scoreA single ranking number balancing bothCatches gaming either metric alone (unlike a plain average)
Common mistake to avoid: reporting only accuracy (or only one of precision/recall) on a skewed dataset. A model can look excellent on accuracy while being functionally useless — always check precision and recall together, and consider F1 when comparing multiple candidates.
