1. Quick recap: where we left off

Part 1 gave you Jtrain, Jcv, and Jtest, plus the train/CV/test split for honest model selection. This part answers the follow-up question: once you have those numbers, how do you read them to figure out what's actually wrong with your model — and what to do about it?

2. Diagnosing bias vs. variance from J_train and J_cv

Back to fitting polynomials to housing prices. A straight line (d=1) underfits; a 4th-order polynomial (d=4) overfits; a quadratic (d=2) is "just right."

DegreeJ_trainJ_cvDiagnosis
d = 1 (linear)HighHighHigh bias — underfitting
d = 2 (quadratic)LowLowNeither — good fit
d = 4LowHighHigh variance — overfitting

The key signatures to memorize:

High bias (underfit): Jtrain is high, and Jtrain ≈ Jcv

High variance (overfit): Jcv ≫ Jtrain (Jtrain may even be low)

Plotting Jtrain and Jcv against the polynomial degree makes this vivid: Jtrain keeps falling as degree increases (a more flexible model always fits the training data at least as well), while Jcv traces a U-shape — high on the left (underfitting), dipping in the middle, then rising again on the right (overfitting).

Why this matters: with one feature you could just look at the curve to see under/overfitting. This numeric signature (comparing Jtrain and Jcv) gives you the exact same diagnosis even with 50 features you could never plot.

3. Can you have both at once?

For a single linear model on one feature, no — you're essentially always on one side of the U-curve or the other. But it is possible, especially with neural networks, to have high bias and high variance simultaneously.

High bias AND high variance: Jtrain is high, and Jcv ≫ Jtrain

Intuitively, this looks like a model that overfits part of the input space (chasing noise in one region) while underfitting another part (too crude to capture the pattern elsewhere). It's rare for most applications — you'll usually have predominantly one problem or the other — but it's worth knowing the signature so you don't misdiagnose it.

4. Regularization's effect on bias and variance

The regularization parameter λ controls the same underfit/overfit trade-off as polynomial degree — just from the opposite direction.

  • λ very large (e.g. 10,000): the model is forced to keep every wj near zero, so f(x) collapses toward a flat constant. High bias — underfits. Jtrain high, Jcv high.
  • λ = 0 (no regularization): a 4th-order polynomial is free to wiggle through every training point. High variance — overfits. Jtrain low, Jcv high.
  • λ intermediate: balances fitting the data against keeping weights small. Jtrain low, Jcv low.

You choose λ with cross validation exactly like you chose the degree in Part 1: try a range (0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, ... doubling up to ~10), fit w,b for each, compute Jcv for each, and pick the λ with the lowest Jcv. Report Jtest only for that final chosen λ.

Analogy: think of λ as a leash length on the model. A very short leash (λ huge) barely lets the model move from a flat line — it can't reach the data (high bias). No leash at all (λ=0) lets it run wild and chase every noisy point (high variance). You want a leash long enough to reach the real pattern but short enough to stay off the noise.

Plotting Jtrain and Jcv against λ gives a curve that's a mirror image of the degree-vs-error plot from Section 2: here, high variance sits on the left (small λ) and high bias sits on the right (large λ) — the opposite ordering from the polynomial-degree plot, where variance was on the right. Same U-shape logic, opposite axis direction.

5. Establishing a baseline: is "high" actually high?

So far "Jtrain is high" has been treated as an obvious red flag. But high compared to what? For messy, real-world data (like audio), even a perfect model can't reach 0% error.

Example: a speech recognition system gets Jtrain = 10.8% and Jcv = 14.8%. At first glance, 10.8% training error looks bad — surely that's high bias? But fluent human transcribers, listening to the same noisy audio clips, also make about 10.6% error. The audio itself is often too noisy for anyone — human or algorithm — to transcribe perfectly.

Gap 1 = Jtrain − baseline   →   large gap = high bias

Gap 2 = Jcv − Jtrain   →   large gap = high variance
BaselineJ_trainJ_cvGap 1 (bias signal)Gap 2 (variance signal)Diagnosis
Case A10.6%10.8%14.8%0.2% — small4.0% — largeHigh variance
Case B10.6%15.0%15.5%4.4% — large0.5% — smallHigh bias
Case C10.6%15.0%19.7%4.4% — large4.7% — largeHigh bias and high variance

Notice Case A is the exact same 10.8%/14.8% numbers that looked like "high bias" at a glance — but once benchmarked against the 10.6% human baseline, it's clearly a variance problem instead: the model is basically matching human performance on training data, so the real issue is the 4-point gap to Jcv.

Why this matters: without a baseline, "10.8% error" is just a number — you can't tell if that's excellent (matching human noise-floor performance) or terrible (a model that should be doing much better). The baseline turns bias/variance diagnosis from a guess into an actual measurement.

Where do you get a baseline?

  • Human-level performance — especially useful for unstructured data like audio, images, or text, where humans are naturally very good.
  • A competing or prior implementation — a previous system, a competitor's benchmark.
  • Experience-based guesses — an estimate from prior projects on similar data.
Note: sometimes the baseline genuinely is 0% (if perfect performance is achievable and expected) — but for noisy real-world data, expecting 0% is unrealistic, and a baseline saves you from chasing an error rate no model can reach.

6. Learning curves

A learning curve plots Jtrain and Jcv as a function of training set size (mtrain) rather than degree or λ. This shows you how the algorithm improves (or doesn't) as it sees more data.

Why does Jtrain go UP as training set grows? With just 1–3 points, a quadratic can fit them almost perfectly (Jtrain ≈ 0). As you add more points, it becomes harder to fit every single one exactly, so training error creeps up. Meanwhile Jcv goes down as the model, trained on more examples, generalizes better. The two curves converge but Jcv stays above Jtrain, since the model always has an "easier time" on data it was fit to.

High bias learning curve

A straight-line fit can only get so good no matter how much data you feed it. Both Jtrain and Jcv flatten out (plateau) at a level well above the baseline/human performance, and — critically — adding more data barely moves either curve further right on the plot.

Key implication: if your algorithm has high bias, getting more training data will not, by itself, help much. The model is too simple to benefit from more examples — you need to increase its capacity instead (see Section 7).

High variance learning curve

Here there's a large, persistent gap between Jcv (high) and Jtrain (low, sometimes even below the human baseline — the model has essentially memorized the training set). Extrapolating the curves to the right, Jcv keeps trending down toward Jtrain as mtrain grows.

Why this matters: this is the mirror-image implication — if your algorithm has high variance, more training data is genuinely likely to help, since the curves are still converging rather than flat.
Note: plotting real learning curves means retraining your model on many different subset sizes (100 examples, 200, 300, ...), which is computationally expensive and not always done in practice — but even just picturing what the curve would look like is a genuinely useful mental habit for reasoning about whether more data will help.

7. The six fixes, sorted by what they actually fix

Revisiting the original debugging checklist from Part 1 — now that you can diagnose bias vs. variance, each option maps cleanly to one problem or the other:

OptionFixesWhy
Get more training examplesHigh varianceLets the model generalize instead of memorizing a small set — no help if the model is already too simple to fit
Try a smaller set of featuresHigh varianceLess flexibility to fit noise (similar to removing some x², x³, ... terms)
Get additional featuresHigh biasGives the model information it structurally lacks to fit even the training data (e.g. adding bedrooms/floors/age, not just size)
Add polynomial featuresHigh biasIncreases model flexibility so it can actually fit the training set
Decrease λHigh biasLess penalty on w → more freedom to fit the training data
Increase λHigh varianceMore penalty on w → smoother, less wiggly function
Common mistake to avoid: if you have a high-bias problem, reducing the size of your training set will NOT help — it might even make Jtrain look artificially better while making Jcv worse. Never throw away training examples to try to fix bias.

8. Bias/variance for neural networks

Before neural networks, ML practitioners had to constantly trade off bias against variance — a model complex enough to avoid underfitting risked overfitting, and vice versa (the classic "bias-variance tradeoff"). Large neural networks, combined with large datasets, largely sidestep this tradeoff.

Why this matters: the core fact enabling this is that large neural networks are low-bias machines — if the network is big enough, it can almost always fit the training set well (as long as the training set isn't enormous). This decouples the two problems: you can attack bias and variance one at a time instead of trading them against each other.

The practical recipe

  1. Train the network, then check: does it do well on the training set (Jtrain low relative to baseline)?
  2. If no — high bias — make the network bigger (more layers or more units per layer), and go back to step 1.
  3. If yes, check: does it do well on the cross-validation set (Jcv close to Jtrain)?
  4. If no — high variance — get more data, and go back to step 1.
  5. If yes to both — done.
Limitations of this recipe: bigger networks get computationally expensive to train — at some point training time becomes infeasible even with GPUs. And you can only collect so much more data before that becomes the bottleneck instead.

Does a bigger network risk more variance?

Perhaps surprisingly: no, generally not — as long as you regularize it appropriately. A well-regularized large network will typically do as well as or better than a smaller one. The main real cost of going bigger is computational (slower training and inference), not accuracy.

J(W,b) = (1/m) ∑ L(f(x(i)),y(i))  +  (λ/2m) ∑all weights W w2
Note: as with linear/logistic regression, you typically regularize the weights W but not the bias term b — in practice this makes very little difference either way. 
layer_1 = Dense(units=25, activation="relu", kernel_regularizer=L2(0.01))
layer_2 = Dense(units=15, activation="relu", kernel_regularizer=L2(0.01))
layer_3 = Dense(units=1,  activation="sigmoid", kernel_regularizer=L2(0.01))
model = Sequential([layer_1, layer_2, layer_3])

Adding kernel_regularizer=L2(λ) to each layer applies the same λ across the network (you could use different values per layer, but a single shared value is simpler and common).

Note on the recipe's implication for practice: once a network is large enough, you're much more often fighting a variance problem than a bias problem — which is part of why bigger models plus more data has been such a successful recipe in deep learning generally.
