1. What is clustering?

Every algorithm from Courses 1 and 2 was supervised learning: you had a training set with input features and a target label Y, and the algorithm's job was to learn a mapping from X to Y. Clustering is your first unsupervised learning algorithm — you're given only inputs X, with no labels at all, and the job is to find some structure or pattern in the data on its own.

Concretely, a clustering algorithm looks at a dataset and tries to group the examples into clusters — sets of points that are close to (similar to) each other, and relatively far from (dissimilar to) points in other clusters. Nobody tells the algorithm what the "correct" groups are; it has to discover them purely from how the data points are distributed.

Why this matters: this is a genuinely different problem setup from everything so far, not just a new algorithm bolted onto old assumptions. There's no cost function comparing predictions to ground-truth labels, because there are no ground-truth labels. That's also why, as you'll see in Section 8, there often isn't a single "correct" number of clusters — without labels telling you the right answer, what counts as a good clustering can genuinely depend on judgment and downstream purpose.

2. K-means intuition: the two repeating steps

Take a dataset of 30 unlabeled points, and ask K-means to find K = 2 clusters. K-means works by repeating two steps over and over:

  1. Assign points to cluster centroids. Guess two locations to be the centers of the clusters — called cluster centroids — shown as a red cross and a blue cross. (The very first guess is random and typically not very good — that's fine, it's just a starting point.) Then, for every one of the 30 points, check whether it's closer to the red centroid or the blue centroid, and color/assign it accordingly.
  2. Move the cluster centroids. Look at all the points now colored red, and move the red centroid to their average location. Do the same for the blue points and the blue centroid.

Then you just repeat: re-assign every point to whichever (now-moved) centroid is closer, re-color, then re-compute each centroid's new average location. Each pass through these two steps can change some points' colors and shift the centroids a bit more.

The two steps K-means alternates between, per iteration. The centroid's new position (right) sits at the average location of the points assigned to it (left).

Eventually, running these two steps stops changing anything: no point switches color, and no centroid moves. At that point, K-means has converged.

Analogy: think of it like organizing a classroom into study groups by seating proximity, with no assigned leader. You start with two random meeting spots. Everyone walks to whichever spot is closer (Step 1). Then each spot's location updates to the average seat position of everyone who came to it (Step 2). People re-check which spot is now closer and may switch. Repeat until nobody wants to switch anymore — you've settled into stable groups.

3. The K-means algorithm, formally

Notation: cluster centroids are written μ₁, μ₂, ..., μ_K — each one is a vector with the same number of dimensions as your training examples (if each example has n features, each μ is also an n-dimensional vector). c⁽ⁱ⁾ is the index (from 1 to K) of whichever centroid training example x⁽ⁱ⁾ is currently assigned to.

Randomly initialize K cluster centroids μ₁, ..., μ_K

Repeat {
  # Assign points to cluster centroids
  for i = 1 to m:
    c⁽ⁱ⁾ := index k (1 to K) that minimizes ||x⁽ⁱ⁾ − μ_k||²

  # Move cluster centroids
  for k = 1 to K:
    μ_k := average (mean) of points assigned to cluster k
}

The term ||x⁽ⁱ⁾ − μ_k|| is the Euclidean distance (also called the L2 norm) between the example and a candidate centroid — the ordinary straight-line distance. In implementation, it's more convenient to minimize the squared distance, since the centroid that minimizes squared distance is guaranteed to be the same one that minimizes plain distance (squaring is monotonic for non-negative numbers), and squared distance avoids an unnecessary square-root computation.

Worked example for moving a centroid: if cluster 1 currently has training examples 1, 5, 6, and 10 assigned to it, then:

μ₁ = (1/4) [ x⁽¹⁾ + x⁽⁵⁾ + x⁽⁶⁾ + x⁽¹⁰⁾ ]

— the mean of those four (vector-valued) training examples, computed coordinate-by-coordinate (average of all the x₁ values, average of all the x₂ values, and so on for however many features you have).

Corner case — an empty cluster: what happens if, after an assignment step, a centroid ends up with zero points assigned to it? Then Step 2 would be trying to average zero points, which is undefined. The most common fix is simply to eliminate that cluster, leaving you with K−1 clusters. A less common alternative is to randomly reinitialize that one centroid and hope it picks up points on the next round — but eliminating the cluster is the standard practice.

4. K-means on data without clear clusters

K-means is most intuitive on data with visually obvious, well-separated blobs — but it's frequently applied to data where the points vary continuously, with no truly distinct groups at all.

The lecture's example: sizing t-shirts. Collect height/weight data from likely customers — this data typically doesn't form clean separate clusters; height and weight vary smoothly across the population. Running K-means with, say, K = 3 anyway will still partition the data into three regions, and those regions can be used directly as sensible "small," "medium," and "large" sizing groups — using each cluster's centroid as the representative height/weight to design that size around.

Why this matters: this shows K-means isn't only useful when there's a "true" clustering to discover — it's also a practical tool for partitioning continuous data into workable groups for a business or engineering decision, even when there's no ground truth about how many groups "really" exist. This directly sets up Section 8, where choosing K = 3 vs. K = 5 for t-shirt sizes becomes a business trade-off rather than a search for the one correct number.

5. The optimization objective: the distortion cost function

Every supervised algorithm you've seen posed a cost function and minimized it (usually via gradient descent). K-means is no different in spirit — it's also minimizing a specific cost function — it's just using the two-step procedure from Sections 2–3 as its optimization method, instead of gradient descent.

Additional notation: μ_c⁽ⁱ⁾ means "the centroid of whichever cluster example x⁽ⁱ⁾ is currently assigned to." (E.g. if training example 10 is assigned to cluster 2, then c⁽¹⁰⁾ = 2, and μ_c⁽¹⁰⁾ is shorthand for μ₂ — the location of that specific centroid.)

The K-means cost function, sometimes called the distortion function:

J(c⁽¹⁾, ..., c⁽ᵐ⁾, μ₁, ..., μ_K) = (1/m) ∑i=1m ||x⁽ⁱ⁾ − μ_c⁽ⁱ⁾||²

In words: for every training example, take the squared distance between that example and the centroid it's currently assigned to, and average those squared distances across all m examples. K-means is trying to find both the cluster assignments (c⁽¹⁾, ..., c⁽ᵐ⁾) and the centroid locations (μ₁, ..., μ_K) that jointly make this average as small as possible:

minc⁽¹⁾,...,c⁽ᵐ⁾, μ₁,...,μ_K  J(c⁽¹⁾, ..., c⁽ᵐ⁾, μ₁, ..., μ_K)
Note on the name: "distortion" isn't a particularly intuitive name for this cost function, but it's the standard term used in the literature — if you see someone refer to "the distortion" in the context of K-means, this J is what they mean.

6. Why each step of the algorithm reduces the cost

This is the part that explains why the two-step procedure in Sections 2–3 is really gradient-descent-like optimization in disguise, and why it's guaranteed to converge.

Step 1 (assignment) minimizes J with respect to c, holding μ fixed

Suppose you fix the centroid locations and only ask: what's the best value of c⁽ⁱ⁾ for a given point x⁽ⁱ⁾? Since the only term of J that involves c⁽ⁱ⁾ is ||x⁽ⁱ⁾ − μ_c⁽ⁱ⁾||², minimizing it means choosing whichever centroid makes that distance smallest — i.e., assigning the point to its nearest centroid. That's exactly the "color each point by the closer centroid" rule from Sections 2–3, now justified as literally minimizing J.

Step 2 (moving centroids) minimizes J with respect to μ, holding c fixed

Now fix which points belong to which cluster, and ask: what location for a centroid μ_k minimizes the average squared distance to its assigned points? It turns out the mean of those points is provably the location that minimizes this.

Worked numeric example (a cluster with 2 points, initial centroid poorly placed): if the two squared distances from the current centroid location are 1² = 1 and 9² = 81, the average is (1 + 81) / 2 = 41. Moving the centroid to the actual mean of the two points changes both distances to 5 and 5, giving an average of (25 + 25) / 2 = 25 — a smaller value. This isn't a coincidence for this example: the mean is mathematically the location that minimizes the average squared distance to a set of points, which is exactly why Step 2 recomputes the centroid as the average.

Why this matters: because both steps individually only ever decrease (or leave unchanged) the cost function J, K-means is guaranteed to converge — J should never increase from one iteration to the next. This gives you two very practical tools: (1) if you ever observe J going up, there's a bug in your implementation; (2) you can use "J stopped decreasing" as your actual convergence check, rather than guessing how many iterations to run. If J is still decreasing but very slowly, that's a judgment call — similar to gradient descent, running longer might help a little, but it's often reasonable to stop and call it "close enough."

7. Initializing K-means, and the local optima problem

How to actually pick the initial centroids

Always choose K < m (the number of clusters can't exceed the number of training examples — you need at least one example per cluster). The standard initialization method: randomly pick K training examples, and set μ₁, ..., μ_K to be those K examples' locations exactly (rather than arbitrary random points in space, which is how earlier illustrations simplified it for clarity).

The local optima problem

Because the starting centroids are random, different random initializations can lead K-means to converge to genuinely different final clusterings — and not all of them are equally good. With K = 3 on one dataset, one initialization might converge to three well-separated, sensible clusters. A different initialization — say, one that happens to place two of the three initial centroids inside the same natural group and the third elsewhere — can converge to a visibly worse clustering. Both are places where the two-step procedure has stopped improving J (so both are technically "converged"), but they correspond to different values of J: the worse clustering is a local optimum of the distortion cost function, not the global optimum.

Why this matters: Section 6 proved that each step of K-means always decreases J — but that guarantee is local. It guarantees you won't get worse from where you currently are; it says nothing about whether you started somewhere that leads to the best possible outcome overall. This is the direct cost of using an alternating-minimization procedure instead of something with global guarantees — and it's precisely why initialization strategy matters enough to get its own section.

The fix: multiple random initializations

Since you now have a cost function J to actually measure how good a given converged clustering is, you can run K-means multiple times from different random initializations, and simply keep whichever run produced the lowest final J:

For i = 1 to 100 {
  Randomly initialize K-means (pick K random training examples)
  Run K-means to convergence → get c⁽¹⁾,...,c⁽ᵐ⁾, μ₁,...,μ_K
  Compute cost function (distortion) J(c⁽¹⁾,...,c⁽ᵐ⁾, μ₁,...,μ_K)
}

Pick the set of clusters that gave the lowest cost J

A typical number of random initializations to try is somewhere between 50 and 1000 (100 is a common default). Going much beyond ~1000 tends to just cost more compute with diminishing returns, while trying at least 50–100 initializations substantially improves your odds of landing on a good clustering rather than a poor local optimum.

Practical takeaway: in practice, you should almost always run K-means with multiple random initializations rather than a single one — it costs extra compute, but it's a straightforward and effective way to avoid getting stuck at a clearly inferior local minimum.

8. Choosing the number of clusters, K

K-means needs K as an input — but for a lot of real datasets, the "right" number of clusters is genuinely ambiguous. Different people looking at the same scatter plot might reasonably say "2 clusters" or "4 clusters" and both be defensible, precisely because clustering is unsupervised: there's no labeled ground truth telling you the correct K.

The elbow method

One technique from the academic literature: run K-means across a range of values of K, and plot the resulting distortion J as a function of K. J will generally start high (few clusters) and decrease as K increases. If the plot shows a sharp drop up to some K, followed by a much slower decrease afterward, that bend is called the "elbow" — and you'd pick the K at that bend.

Important limitation, stated directly in the lecture: the elbow method is not actually recommended as a go-to technique — the instructor explicitly says he rarely uses it himself. The problem: for a lot of real applications, the J-vs-K curve decreases smoothly with no clear elbow at all, leaving you with nothing clean to pick.
A related mistake to avoid entirely: never choose K purely by minimizing J. Distortion J will (almost) always keep decreasing as K increases — adding more clusters basically always lets you fit the data more tightly. Minimizing J directly would just push you toward choosing the largest possible K, which defeats the purpose of clustering (at the extreme, K = m gives every point its own cluster and J = 0, which is useless).

The actual recommended approach: evaluate on the downstream task

In practice, you're usually running K-means to get clusters you'll actually use for something afterward. The recommended approach is to evaluate different values of K based on how well each one serves that downstream purpose — not based on the clustering metric alone.

Back to the t-shirt sizing example: running K-means with K = 3 gives you S/M/L sizing; K = 5 gives you XS/S/M/L/XL. Both are perfectly valid clusterings of the same data — neither is more "correct" in a mathematical sense. The actual decision comes down to a business trade-off: more sizes (larger K) means a better fit for more people, but also higher manufacturing and shipping costs from producing more distinct sizes. You'd run K-means with both K = 3 and K = 5, look at the resulting clusters, and pick based on that fit-vs-cost trade-off — not based on which one has lower distortion.

Why this matters: this is the throughline of the whole choosing-K discussion — since clustering has no ground-truth answer to check against, "good" is defined by usefulness for whatever you're actually trying to accomplish with the clusters, not by any purely internal statistical criterion. The same logic will resurface later in the course (e.g. K-means applied to image compression involves a similar quality-vs-size trade-off when picking K).
